# Step 2: Dataset Architecture & Data Generation

**Project:** Customer Revenue Intelligence: An Interactive Data Science Platform for Customer Segmentation, Revenue Prediction and Business Growth

> **SYNTHETIC DATA.** Every record in this dataset was generated by `scripts/generate_dataset.py` (random seed 42) for academic demonstration. No real customers, companies or transactions are represented. Customer names are random pairings of common Indian first names and surnames. Product names are generic and belong to no real brand.

This document keeps two kinds of statement separate:

- **Design assumption:** a rule written into the generator before it ran.
- **Generated result:** a number read from the output after the code ran.

Everything in the "Generated result" boxes comes from actually running the code. Re-running the code with seed 42 reproduces those numbers exactly: two runs produced files with the same MD5 checksum.

## Part A: Dataset Design

| # | Item | Design |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Business scenario** | A fictional, unnamed, mid-sized, multi-category Indian online retailer. It is deliberately unnamed so it cannot be confused with your internship company. It sells electronics, fashion, home & kitchen, beauty, sports and books to customers in 34 Indian cities. It has grown quickly since 2023, but its marketing is still mass-market: every customer gets the same offers. |
| 2 | **Dataset purpose** | Supply one realistic transaction history that can support every later step: SQL, EDA, RFM, K-Means, CLV, revenue prediction, forecasting, Power BI and Streamlit. Business patterns are built in so that each of those analyses has something real to find. |
| 3 | **Unit of observation** | **One order line**: one product within one customer order. One order (checkout) can hold 1–3 different products. |
| 4 | **Primary key** | `Order_Line_ID` (e.g. `OL-0000001`). `Order_ID` alone is not unique, because a multi-product order has several rows. |
| 5 | **Foreign keys** (after normalisation, Part K) | `Order_ID` → orders, `Customer_ID` → customers, `Product_ID` → products, (City, State) → locations |
| 6 | **Customers** | 2,000 (exact, by design) |
| 7 | **Products** | 61 across 26 sub-categories |
| 8 | **Categories** | 6: Electronics, Fashion, Home & Kitchen, Beauty, Sports, Books |
| 9 | **Regions** | 5 (North, South, East, West, Central), covering 21 states/UTs and 34 cities |
| 10 | **Time period** | 1 Jan 2023 – 31 Dec 2025: 36 months of daily order dates |

> **Generated result:** the raw file has **18,819 rows × 23 columns**. That is 18,670 unique order lines plus 149 injected duplicate rows. The lines belong to **13,251 orders** placed by 2,000 customers for 61 products.

**Note on the Step 1 blueprint.** The blueprint estimated about 12,000 rows, 60 products and about 15 states. The final design produces more rows because multi-product orders are included, and it covers 21 states to give the regional analysis better coverage. The raw file is named `customer_sales_raw.csv`, as your Step 2 brief specifies. The generator lives in `scripts/`, also per the Step 2 brief, rather than in `src/`.

## Part C: Realistic Data Relationships (design assumptions)

| Relationship | How it is built into the generator |
|---|---|
| **Sales** = Qty × Price × (1 − Discount) | Computed exactly and rounded to 2 decimals |
| **Cost** = Qty × Price × cost ratio | Each product has a fixed cost ratio (±1% supplier noise). Cost is based on the **list** price, so a discount reduces Sales but not Cost. |
| **Profit** = Sales − Cost | Heavy discounts on thin-margin items produce negative profit |
| Different price ranges | Prices run from ₹299 (storybook) to ₹84,999 (laptop) |
| Different category patterns | Electronics: high ticket, thin margin (cost ratio 0.45–0.925). Fashion: high margin, highest return rate (11%). Beauty: low ticket, highest margin. Books: multi-unit baskets. |
| Price inflation | List prices rise about 5% per year, with ±2.5% listing noise |
| Discounts respond to sales events | Category base discount, plus 7 points during sale events and 12 points during the Diwali festive sale, in coupon tiers of 5%, capped at 40%. Around 30% of non-sale purchases pay full price. |
| Discounts drive volume | At a discount of 25% or more, there is a 15% chance of one extra unit (not applied to electronics) |
| Age drives category preference | 18–24: fashion, electronics, beauty. 35–49: home & kitchen. 50+: home & kitchen and books. |
| City tier drives price preference | Metro (Tier 1) customers lean slightly towards pricier items; Tier 3 towards cheaper ones |
| Payment habits | Each customer has a preferred method, used in 70% of their orders. Younger customers favour UPI/Wallet; older ones COD, Card and Net Banking. UPI share rises and COD share falls over time. |
| Cancellations and returns | 3.5% base cancellation rate, +3 points for COD. Return rate varies by category, +2 points for COD, +2 points for bargain-driven customers. |
| Regions differ | Customer share: South 27%, West 26%, North 22%, East 13%, Central 12%. New-customer acquisition grows fastest in the South and East and slows in Central. |
| Seasonality | Monthly factors, a weekend lift (Fri–Sun), and five Indian sale events a year: Republic Day, mid-year sale, Independence Day, the Diwali festive sale and the year-end sale |
| Moving festival | The festive sale runs for the 24 days before Diwali (12 Nov 2023, 1 Nov 2024, 20 Oct 2025), so the peak shifts between October and November. This is a realistic complication for forecasting. |
| Non-linear trend | Growth through 2023, a slowdown in April–July 2024, recovery in Q4 2024, then flatter growth in 2025. The trend is deliberately not perfectly increasing. |
| Repeat and existing customers | Some customers were already buying before January 2023. The design share varies by persona: 70% of high-value frequent, 60% of loyal, 50% of at-risk, 40% of inactive, 35% of occasional, 30% of low-value and 0% of new customers. Their first order in this extract is therefore "Returning", because the source system knows their earlier history. *Generated result:* 38.6% of all customers. |

> **Generated result (generator self-check, not EDA).** These were checked only to confirm that the rules took effect.
> - Within every category, margin falls as the discount deepens. For example, the Fashion margin drops from 52% at no discount to 25% at a 30–40% discount.
> - About 1.5% of lines are loss-making.
> - Quarterly Delivered revenue peaks in Q4 of each year and dips in Q2 2024.
>
> Full analysis of these patterns belongs to Step 4 (EDA).

## Part D: Customer Behaviour (hidden personas)

Each customer is secretly assigned one of seven behavioural personas. The persona controls:

- how many orders the customer places,
- when they first and last buy,
- which price points they prefer,
- how discount-hungry they are,
- how many items they put in a basket.

**The persona is never saved.** Customer IDs are shuffled so that the ID reveals nothing. Segments must be *rediscovered* in Steps 5–6 through RFM and K-Means.

| Persona (hidden) | Share | Lifetime orders | Last order before 31 Dec 2025 | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-value frequent | 6% | 18–30 | 0–40 days | Prefers premium items, bigger baskets, low discount need |
| Loyal | 14% | 8–15 | 0–75 days | Steady, mid-to-premium spend |
| New | 12% | 1–3 | First purchase Jul–Dec 2025 | Young, still exploring |
| Occasional | 25% | 3–7 | 20–240 days | Buys mostly in sale periods |
| At-risk | 13% | 6–12 | **120–300 days** | Was a good customer, has gone quiet |
| Low-value | 18% | 2–5 | 0–300 days | Cheap items, deep discounts, strongly sale-driven, higher returns |
| Inactive | 12% | 1–3 | **> 14 months** | Bought early, never came back |

The personas overlap on purpose, just as real customers do. For example, a low-value customer can be recent, and an occasional customer can look at-risk. RFM and K-Means will therefore find meaningful but imperfect groups, which is realistic and good for viva discussion.

## Part E: Product Structure

61 products across 6 categories and 26 sub-categories. Each product has a base price, a **cost ratio** (unit cost ÷ list price) and a **popularity weight**.

| Category | Sub-categories | Products | Price range (₹) | Cost ratio (margin before discount) | Design role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronics | Smartphones, Laptops, Televisions, Audio, Wearables, Accessories | 13 | 1,299 – 84,999 | 0.45 – 0.925 | **High revenue, low margin.** The laptop and TV lines barely break even. |
| Fashion | Men's Clothing, Women's Clothing, Footwear, Fashion Accessories | 11 | 799 – 3,499 | 0.38 – 0.55 | High margin, highest return rate |
| Home & Kitchen | Kitchen Appliances, Cookware, Home Decor, Storage | 11 | 699 – 7,499 | 0.50 – 0.73 | Mid margin, favoured by 35+ customers |
| Beauty | Skincare, Haircare, Makeup, Grooming | 9 | 349 – 1,799 | 0.35 – 0.62 | Low ticket, **highest margin**, frequent repurchase |
| Sports | Fitness Equipment, Cycling, Team Sports, Outdoor | 9 | 699 – 14,999 | 0.50 – 0.78 | Includes deliberate slow movers (camping tent, bicycle) |
| Books | Fiction, Non-Fiction, Academic, Children | 8 | 299 – 1,499 | 0.60 – 0.70 | Low revenue, multi-unit baskets |

With this structure the later analysis can identify:

- **best-sellers** (high popularity × moderate price),
- **most profitable products** (fat margin × volume),
- **low performers** (popularity weight 0.08–0.4),
- **high-revenue but low-margin categories** (Electronics),
- the **revenue-vs-profit gap** (Business Questions 3 and 4).

The full catalogue is in `CATALOGUE` at the top of `scripts/generate_dataset.py`.

## Part G: Time Structure

- **36 months** (Jan 2023 – Dec 2025) of daily order dates. That gives 36 monthly points for forecasting, with the last 6 months available as a holdout.
- **Demand weight per day** = business trend × month seasonality × weekday factor × sale-event multiplier.
  - Trend: growth, then a mid-2024 slowdown, then recovery, then a 2025 plateau.
  - Month seasonality: February low; October and November high.
  - Weekday factor: Friday to Sunday up 5–12%.
  - Sale-event multipliers: 1.15 to 1.60.
- **Sale-driven personas** (occasional, low-value) weight these days more heavily, so their purchases cluster in sale windows.
- **Customer acquisition** (first purchase dates) follows the same calendar plus each region's growth curve.
- **No perfect trend.** The monthly revenue series has a dip, festive spikes whose timing moves, and a flattening final year.

## Part I: Dataset Generation (code)

**File:** `scripts/generate_dataset.py`

It covers all nine requirements:

1. Generates the data.
2. Fixes the seed (`config.RANDOM_SEED = 42`), with a second stream (seed 43) for the data-quality injection.
3. Builds the relationships in Parts C–G.
4. Injects the Part H issues.
5. Saves the CSV.
6. Prints the dataset shape.
7. Prints the first 10 rows.
8. Prints the data types.
9. Prints basic statistics.

Faker is **not** used. Curated Indian name lists keep the output identical on every machine, whatever Faker version is installed, and remove a dependency.

> **Generated result (console output):**
> - Shape: 18,819 rows × 23 columns.
> - Dtypes as read from the CSV:
>   - `Unit_Price` loads as **text** (`str`/`object`) because of the injected `₹1,299` values.
>   - `Customer_Age` loads as **float** because missing values force floats.

## Part K: SQL Preparation

### Choice: Option B, a normalised relational model, plus one flat view

| Criterion | Option A: one flat table | Option B: normalised tables ✔ |
|---|---|---|
| Demonstrates relational design (PK/FK, 3NF) | ✗ | ✓ An MBA Data Science viva expects this |
| JOIN / GROUP BY / window-function practice | Limited | ✓ Realistic multi-table queries |
| Data integrity | None | ✓ CHECK and FK constraints reject bad rows |
| Redundancy | Customer and product details repeated about 18k times | Stored once |
| Matches Power BI star schema | Would need splitting in Power BI anyway | ✓ customers / products / locations become dimensions; order_items becomes the fact table |
| Ease for pandas and Streamlit | ✓ | ✓ via `vw_sales_flat`, which recreates the flat 23-column table |

Option B gets the best of both. SQL work shows proper modelling, while Python, Power BI and Streamlit can still read one flat view.

### Schema (`sql/schema.sql`, SQLite dialect with PostgreSQL notes)

```
locations (location_id PK, city, state, region)
    1 ──< customers (customer_id PK, customer_name, customer_age, gender, location_id FK)
              1 ──< orders (order_id PK, customer_id FK, order_date, payment_method, customer_type)
                        1 ──< order_items (order_line_id PK, order_id FK, product_id FK,
                                           quantity, unit_price, discount, sales, cost, profit, order_status)
products (product_id PK, product_name, category, sub_category) 1 ──< order_items
VIEW vw_sales_flat = all five tables joined back into the 23 original columns
```

- **Constraints:** discount 0–0.40, quantity > 0, age 18–100, allowed category/region/status values, the Sales and Profit formulas (±₹1), and foreign keys.
- **Indexes:** on every FK plus `order_date` and `order_status`.
- **Payment "Unknown":** allowed in `orders.payment_method`, for the blanks Step 3 cannot recover.
- **Grain decisions:**
  - Unit price is stored per order line, not per product, because prices change over time.
  - Payment method and customer type belong to the order.
  - Status belongs to the line, because a customer can return one item from an order.

> **Generated result (schema test):** the schema was tested by loading the *pre-injection* data into SQLite.
> - All 5 tables loaded: 34 locations, 2,000 customers, 61 products, 13,251 orders and 18,670 order items.
> - There were zero foreign-key violations.
> - `vw_sales_flat` reproduced the source values exactly.
> - A raw-style bad row (quantity −1) was rejected by the CHECK constraint.
>
> The actual database file is built in Step 3, after cleaning.
